# Researchers: real people behind the Japan knowledge

Bios of real anthropologists, ethnologists, archaeologists, geographers and
ethnomusicologists whose work on Japan and the Ainu informs this game.
Kept here so that in-game knowledge (geography, kamuy, music, naming) has
citable sources and does not come from thin air. Where a claim in the game
comes from one of these people, this is where to check it.

Family names are given in capitals. "Most famous work" is listed for each
entry, together with the other publications the game is most likely to
draw on.

---

## 1. Ruth BENEDICT (1887–1948) — the outsider's classic

American cultural anthropologist, born in New York, PhD under Franz Boas at
Columbia University, where she later taught and where Margaret Mead was a
friend and colleague. She was one of the founders of the "culture and
personality" school — the idea that a culture has a describable
psychological character with which its members are raised — and she worked
on the Zuñi, the Cochiti and the Blackfoot before Japan. During the Second
World War she worked for the US Office of War Information, and it was from
that vantage point, with fieldwork in Japan impossible, that she wrote her
Japan study.

**Most famous publication:** *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword: Patterns of
Japanese Culture* (1946) — the single most widely read book ever written
about Japan, based not on fieldwork but on wartime interviews with
Japanese Americans, on Japanese literature and film. It popularised ideas
still in circulation today: obligation (*giri*) and duty (*gimu*), the
chrysanthemum-and-sword duality of beauty and violence, and the claim that
Japan is a "shame culture" rather than a "guilt culture".

**Other work:** *Patterns of Culture* (1934), still a classic of American
anthropology and the origin of the "culture is a pattern" style of
writing; *Zuni Mythology* (1935); *Race: Science and Politics* (1940), an
early and influential attack on scientific racism.

**Why she matters here:** she is the starting point, not the last word.
Her work is the textbook example of how easily "Japan" gets described as
one uniform thing — exactly the trap the game should avoid when it teaches
about a country with Ainu, Ryukyuan and immigrant histories. That her
famous book was written with no fieldwork at all is also a useful reminder
for a game: confident description is not the same as knowledge.

## 2. Emiko OHNUKI-TIERNEY (b. 1934) — symbol and identity

Born in Kobe, Japan, trained in Japan and in the United States, long a
professor at the University of Wisconsin–Madison (William F. Vilas
Research Professor), and a member of the American Academy of Arts and
Sciences. She is unusual in combining long ethnographic fieldwork with
structural-symbolic analysis, and unusual in having worked on both sides
of the Japan/Ainu divide: her early fieldwork was with Ainu communities
resettled from Sakhalin, in Hokkaido, in the 1960s and 1970s, before she
turned to the symbols of majority Japanese identity.

**Most famous publication:** *The Monkey as Mirror: Symbolic
Transformations in Japanese History and Ritual* (1987), a study of how the
same animal figure can carry opposite meanings — sacred messenger in one
period, scapegoat and self-mockery in another — as a way of tracking
Japanese self-understanding across history.

**Other work:** *Illness and Healing among the Sakhalin Ainu: A Symbolic
Interpretation* (1981), the Ainu ethnography that grounds her later
method; *Rice as Self: Japanese Identities through Time* (1993), on rice
as the "self" of Japan against the outside world; *Kamikaze Diaries:
Reflections of Japanese Student Soldiers* (2006), which reads the
students' own letters and diaries and shows them as educated, critical
readers rather than fanatics — an argument with real moral force; and
*Flowers That Kill: Communicative Opacity in Political Spaces* (2015) on
how symbols are used to say and hide at the same time.

**Why she matters here:** she is the model for how survivalm should treat
symbols — a plant, a name or a ritual is never "just" decoration; it
carries identity, and she shows how to read that without flattening it.

## 3. Joy HENDRY (b. 1951) — wrapping, politeness, presentation

British social anthropologist, long associated with Oxford Brookes
University, where she taught for decades and trained a generation of
anthropologists of Japan in Britain. She did her fieldwork in a Kyushu
town and has been one of the most consistent writers of accessible,
fieldwork-based books on Japanese society.

**Most famous publication:** *Wrapping Culture: Politeness, Presentation
and Power in Japan and Other Societies* (1993), which reads gift wrapping,
packaging, bowing, levels of polite language and even architecture as one
grammar of "wrapping" — layers of presentation that communicate respect,
intimacy or distance, and therefore power.

**Other work:** *Becoming Japanese: The World of the Pre-School Child*
(1986), an early study of how small children are taught social
belonging; *Understanding Japanese Society* (1987, and later editions), a
standard university textbook and probably the most assigned introduction
to the subject in English; *An Anthropologist in Japan: Glimpses of Life
in the Field* (1999), a candid account of doing the fieldwork itself;
*The Orient Strikes Back: A Global View of Cultural Display* (2000), on
Japanese theme parks and cultural exhibitions abroad; *Sharing Our
Worlds* (2008), an introduction to anthropology for students.

**Why she matters here:** gives the game a way to teach visible Japanese
form without a lecture — layers, wrapping, thresholds and ceremony read as
meaning, which fits shrine precincts, packaging, and the layered structure
of a commemorative world. Her fieldwork-style writing is also a model for
tone: precise about practice, modest about conclusions.

## 4. Neil Gordon MUNRO (1863–1942) — the resident archaeologist of Ainu life

Scottish physician, born in Birkenhead, who went to Japan in the early
1890s and stayed almost fifty years, for many years medical director of
the Yokohama General Hospital. He was one of the first people to excavate
Japan's **prehistoric shell mounds**, and later — in the 1930s — he moved
to **Nibutani, in Hokkaido**, and lived among the Ainu, recording their
daily life, beliefs and material culture at first hand. He died in Japan
in 1942; his collections survive in Japanese museums and his Ainu
photographs are still used by researchers and by Ainu communities today.

**Most famous publication:** *Ainu Creed and Cult* (completed with B. Z.
Seligman and H. Watanabe, published posthumously in 1962) — the first
detailed account of Ainu religion and ritual written by a non-Japanese,
and still cited for the bear ceremony and the *kamuy* system.

**Other work:** *Coins of Japan* (1904), a numismatic study that is still
a standard reference; *Prehistoric Japan* (1908), an early synthesis of
Japanese archaeology by a foreigner working in the country.

**Why he matters here:** the Ainu religion material in §4 of
`research.md` (kamuy, iyomante, inau) rests on recorders like him. He is
also a caution: he documented a living culture as it was being displaced,
and his framing was of his time. For the game, he is the model of the
resident scholar who learns the language and stays — and of the ethical
problem that comes with it.

## 5. Junko HABU (b. 1958) — Jomon archaeology, the deep past

Japanese archaeologist and anthropologist, professor at the University of
California, Berkeley, after earlier posts in Japan and at Northwestern.
She works on **Jomon hunter-gatherers** (ca. 14,000–500 BC) — the
long-lived, sedentary, pottery-making foragers of the Japanese
archipelago — combining excavation with ecological modelling,
household-level analysis of settlements, and the question of how a
hunter-gatherer society sustained itself for millennia without farming.
She also writes on cultural heritage, on the politics of archaeological
interpretation, and on community-based archaeology in Japan.

**Most famous publication:** *Ancient Jomon of Japan* (Cambridge
University Press, 2004) — the standard English-language synthesis of the
period, and the book that brought Jomon archaeology into international
debates about complex hunter-gatherers.

**Other work:** *Subsistence-Settlement Systems and Intersite Variability
in the Early Jomon Period of Japan* (2001); the co-edited volume
*Evaluating Multiple Narratives: Beyond Nationalist, Colonialist,
Imperialist Archaeologies* (2008), which takes the nationalist history of
East Asian archaeology — including Japan's — as its object; and the
*Handbook of East and Southeast Asian Archaeology* (co-edited, 2017).

**Why she matters here:** the deeper past the game sits on. If survivalm
ever gestures at who lived on the land before any named group — or at how
a small community sustains itself in a forested archipelago — her work is
the sober reference, and she is one of the younger generations still
producing it.

## 6. Yuji MURAYAMA (b. 1961) — the geographer of how Japan is mapped

Japanese geographer working in human geography and GIScience, professor
emeritus at the University of Tsukuba, former director of its Division of
Spatial Information Science, and a member of the Science Council of Japan.
His research concerns **land-use change, urbanisation and geospatial
analysis of Japan** — how Japanese cities, regions and rural areas have
been mapped, measured and transformed over the modern period — together
with the methods (remote sensing, spatial statistics, GIS) by which that
can be done rigorously. He is a generation younger than the classic
Japanologists in this file.

**Most famous publication:** *Progress in Geospatial Analysis* (editor,
Springer, 2012), a widely used overview of spatial-analysis methods and
applications, alongside the earlier edited volume *Japanese Urban System*
(2000), still a standard reference on how the country's urban network is
structured.

**Other work:** *Urban Development in Asia and Africa* (co-edited, 2017);
ongoing work on shrinking and ageing Japanese regions, which is where
geography in Japan now does much of its most consequential work; and a
number of Japanese-language textbooks on spatial analysis with GIS.

**Why he matters here:** the whole premise of survivalm is a map — Japan
redrawn at 1:1000 by hand in `world/island.gd`. MURAYAMA is the
practitioner of exactly that discipline in the real country: how land is
classified, measured and turned into a picture, and what is lost when it
is. He is the reference for doing it deliberately rather than
decoratively.

## 7. Tadao UMESAO (1920–2010) — civilisation as a chain of landscapes

Japanese anthropologist and ethnologist, professor for decades at Kyoto
University and the founder and first director-general of Japan's National
Museum of Ethnology (**Minpaku**, Suita, opened 1974) — the institution
that put Japanese ethnology on the world map and still holds the
photographic archives of the Kyoto University Scientific Expeditions he
helped lead. His early fieldwork was with pastoralists in Mongolia and
Central Asia, which shaped his lifelong habit of comparing Japan not with
"the West" but with the rest of Asia. He was also an early and
enthusiastic advocate of computing and information science in the
humanities in Japan.

**Most famous publication:** *An Ecological View of History: Japanese
Civilization in the World Context* (first published as an essay in
*Chūō Kōron* in 1957; English translation by Beth Cary, with a preface by
Harumi Befu, 2003) — the argument that world history can be read
ecologically, as a belt of civilisations running from Europe through the
Middle East, India, Southeast Asia and East Asia to Japan, each shaped by
its landscape and climate rather than by a single line of progress. It
reframed Japan as one link in an Asian chain instead of an exception.

**Why he matters here:** he is the liveliest intellectual fit for the
game's premise. A world built to commemorate a country is a landscape
argument — terrain treated as memory — and UMESAO spent his career
insisting that landscape shapes what a civilisation becomes and how it
sees itself. If the puzzle layer of survivalm ever explains *why* the
world looks like Japan and not somewhere else, it is his question it is
answering. (He is also, usefully, the reason Japan has a national museum
of ethnology at all — a real-world precedent for a world built out of
comparison.)

## 8. Hirokazu MIYAZAKI (b. 1968) — hope as method

Japanese-born (Tokyo) cultural anthropologist, professor at Cornell
University, where he directed the East Asia Program and later the Mario
Einaudi Center for International Studies; trained in anthropology in Japan
and at the Australian National University. He is one of the most
influential Japanese-born anthropologists of his generation, and is known
for turning a philosophical problem into an ethnographic method rather
than a theory.

**Most famous publication:** *The Method of Hope: Anthropology,
Philosophy, and Fijian Knowledge* (2004) — a study of the Suvavou people
of Fiji and their long pursuit of a land claim, which asks how people keep
hope alive when the outcome is not in their hands. The book's argument is
that hope is not an emotion but a technique, visible in the way people
reorganise knowledge and repeat their claims.

**Other work:** *Arbitraging Japan: Dreams of Capitalism at the End of
Finance* (2013), an ethnography of Tokyo derivatives traders before and
after the financial crisis; the co-edited volume *The Economy of Hope*
(2017); and recent work on **Ainu land rights and heritage in Hokkaido**,
including the politics around the Upopoy national museum — plus essays on
Japanese society facing disaster, decline and what a life worth living
might look like afterwards, which he writes about as a public voice in
Japan as well as a scholar.

**Why he matters here:** he is the bridge between the game's premise and
the present. A world built to commemorate a country is an act of hope —
that something survives being remembered — and MIYAZAKI is the
anthropologist who has thought hardest about what that actually means,
including for the Ainu, whose commemoration has been contested. He is also
a direct source for how the game should handle Upopoy-era Ainu politics:
as a live claim, not a museum label.

## 9. Chizuko UENO (b. 1948) — Japan's best-known feminist

Japanese sociologist and feminist scholar, born in Toyama Prefecture,
professor emerita of the University of Tokyo after earlier posts at Kyoto
and elsewhere. Often called Japan's "best-known feminist", she writes on
patriarchy, the family, care work, gender and capitalism, and has been a
public intellectual for four decades — a regular on television and in
magazines, and the author of both academic books and bestsellers. Her 2019
University of Tokyo entrance-ceremony speech, in which she told incoming
students that their effort would be rewarded only because they had been
lucky, became internationally famous. She is also one of the country's
**most controversial** scholars: her positions on the "comfort women"
system, the imperial succession and wartime responsibility have made her
the target of sustained nationalist campaigns and of organised
harassment.

**Most famous publication:** *Patriarchy and Capitalism: The Horizon of
Marxist Feminism* (家父長制と資本制, 1990), the book that established
"feminism" as a serious analytic category in Japanese social science, and
arguably her most cited work. In English she is best known through *The
Modern Family in Japan: Its Rise and Fall* (2009), which gathers her
essays on how the post-war family was manufactured and is now
dissolving.

**Other work:** *Nationalism and Gender* (English translation 2004), on
the comfort women system and how national memory is gendered; *Misogyny*
(女ぎらい, 2010), on contempt for women inside and outside feminism; work
on ageing alone (the "*ohitori-sama*" single-life question); and a long
series of edited volumes on gender in Japanese society.

**Why she matters here:** she is the corrective to a game about
traditions. A commemorative Japan made only of torii, haiku and kamuy
freezes the country as beautiful and harmonious; UENO is the scholar who
insists on asking who does the work, who is excluded, and whose memory the
monument serves — questions the game's puzzles should eventually be able
to ask without becoming a lecture.

## 10. Shinji MIYADAI (b. 1959) — the controversial voice on youth and culture

Japanese sociologist, born in Sendai, professor at Tokyo Metropolitan
University, originally trained in sociology with time spent as a film
director's assistant. He became one of Japan's most visible public
intellectuals in the 1990s, writing and speaking about youth, sexuality,
otaku culture, media, and the background to the 1995 Aum Shinrikyō
attacks, in a fast-talking, deliberately provocative style that made him a
television fixture and a permanent argument. He has also been a
**controversial** figure — his writing on teenage sexuality and teenage
"compensated dating" (*enjo kōsai*) drew heavy criticism, as did his
media-savvy handling of subculture. In 2022 he survived a knife attack on
his campus by a man who had written him as a target; the case renewed
debate in Japan about violence against public commentators.

**Most famous publication:** his account of the Aum period and of 1990s
Japan, published in Japanese as *虚構の時代の果て* ("The End of the Age of
Fiction", 1996), which turned the 1995 attacks into evidence about how
Japanese society had come to treat reality as fabricated — a book that
set the terms of Japan's subculture debate for a decade, alongside
*終わりなき日常を生きろ* ("Live in the Endless Everyday", 1998) and
*透明な存在の不透明な悪意* ("The Opaque Malice of Transparent Beings",
1997).

**Other work in English:** "Transformation of Semantics in the History of
Japanese Subcultures since 1992" (*Mechademia*, vol. 6, 2011, translated
by Shion Kono and Thomas Lamarre), the most accessible statement of his
argument about how otaku culture changed the meaning of fiction,
sexuality and community in Japan.

**Why he matters here:** the counterweight to the "traditional Japan"
material. His work shows Japan as contemporary, urban, unsettled and
globally entangled — the register the game should reach for when its
commemorated Japan starts conflicting with the Japan the player thinks
they know. He is also the clearest case in this file of a scholar whose
own reception is part of the history: understanding Japan means
understanding the arguments it has about itself.

## 11. William P. MALM (1928–2024) — the scholar of the shamisen

American ethnomusicologist, professor at the University of Michigan for
his whole career, and the central figure in Western scholarship on
Japanese traditional music. He studied Japanese music in Japan with
teachers of the instruments themselves as well as in the archive, and his
research area was **shamisen (三味線, the three-string plucked lute)** music,
especially *nagauta* — the "long song" that accompanies kabuki. He was
active in the Society for Ethnomusicology (whose oral-history series
records his career) and is the teacher of a large part of the field's next
generation.

**Most famous publication:** *Nagauta: The Heart of Kabuki Music* (1963),
still the standard study of the genre, which analyses the shamisen
ensemble, its vocal lines, its place in the kabuki theatre, and how the
musical structure carries dramatic meaning — one of the first serious
Western treatments of a Japanese musical genre on its own terms.

**Other work:** *Japanese Music and Musical Instruments* (1959), the
standard English survey of the subject, later revised and reissued as
*Traditional Japanese Music and Musical Instruments* (2000);
*Six Hidden Views of Japanese Music* (1986), a set of studies on specific
repertoires and listening habits; and compositions of his own in a
Japanese-influenced idiom.

**Why he matters here:** the shamisen is the instrument most people
recognise as "Japanese music", and its repertoire — nagauta, jiuta,
kabuki, bunraku — is where a commemoration of Japan would most obviously
put music. He is also the methodological model for the game's audio
layer: document an instrument, its repertoire and its social settings
together, not as a sample pack. For the Ainu tonkori and the Ryukyuan
sanshin — the other string traditions the game touches — the same
attention applies.

## Notes

- Selection criteria: real, well-documented researchers on Japan, mixing
  classic and current, Japanese and non-Japanese, with at least one
  archaeologist (MUNRO, HABU) and one geographer (MURAYAMA, UMESAO).
- Women: BENEDICT, OHNUKI-TIERNEY, HENDRY, HABU, UENO. Younger generation:
  HABU (b. 1958), MIYAZAKI (b. 1968).
- Japanese-born: OHNUKI-TIERNEY, HABU, MURAYAMA, UMESAO, MIYAZAKI, UENO,
  MIYADAI.
- Controversial figures: UENO, MIYADAI (both included on purpose — a
  commemorative world should carry its tensions, not just its treasures).
- Non-academics with academic standing: MUNRO (physician by training, the
  first resident recorder of Ainu daily life).
- Spelling note: the instrument is **shamisen** (三味線), not "shamizen";
  the Okinawan relative is the **sanshin** (三線), the Ainu relative the
  **tonkori**. In-game text should use these forms.
- Deliberately omitted: early Meiji-era "Japan hands" whose framing was
  orientalist without fieldwork, and researchers whose main reputations
  rest on discredited work.
- Every Japanese term used in the game should be traceable to a source in
  this file or in `research.md`.
