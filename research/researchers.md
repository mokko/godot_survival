# Researchers: real people behind the Japan knowledge

Bios of real anthropologists, archaeologists, geographers and
ethnomusicologists whose work on Japan and the Ainu informs this game.
Kept here so that in-game knowledge (geography, kamuy, music, naming) has
citable sources and does not come from thin air. Where a claim in the game
comes from one of these people, this is where to check it.

Family names are given in capitals.

---

## 1. Ruth BENEDICT (1887–1948) — the outsider's classic

American cultural anthropologist, student of Franz Boas, one of the
founders of the "culture and personality" school. Her *The Chrysanthemum
and the Sword: Patterns of Japanese Culture* (1946) was written during the
Second World War, when fieldwork in Japan was impossible, so she built it
from interviews with Japanese Americans, literature and film — the famous
"study of a culture at a distance". It shaped Western images of Japan
(obligation, *giri*, shame) for decades.

**Why she matters here:** she is the starting point, not the last word.
Her work is the textbook example of how easily "Japan" gets described as
one uniform thing — exactly the trap the game should avoid when it teaches
about a country with Ainu, Ryukyuan and immigrant histories.

## 2. Emiko OHNUKI-TIERNEY (b. 1934) — symbol and identity

Born in Kobe, Japan; trained in Japan and the United States; long a
professor at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. Rare in combining
ethnographic fieldwork with structural-symbolic analysis. Her early work
was on the **Sakhalin Ainu** (*Illness and Healing among the Sakhalin
Ainu*, 1981); she then wrote on rice as a symbol of Japanese identity, the
monkey as self-image, and — most strikingly — on the diaries of **WWII
kamikaze pilots** (*Kamikaze Diaries*, 2006), reading the young men who
died as educated, critical readers rather than fanatics.

**Why she matters here:** she is the model for how survivalm should treat
symbols — a plant, a name or a ritual is never "just" decoration; it
carries identity, and she shows how to read that without flattening it.

## 3. Joy HENDRY (b. 1951) — wrapping, politeness, presentation

British social anthropologist, long associated with Oxford Brookes
University; a founder of the anthropology of Japan in Britain. *Wrapping
Culture: Politeness, Presentation and Power in Japan and Other Societies*
(1993) reads gift wrapping, packaging, bowing, language levels and even
architecture as one grammar of "wrapping" — layers of presentation that
communicate respect and social position. Later books (*The Orient Strikes
Back*, on Japanese theme parks abroad) track Japan's cultural exports.

**Why she matters here:** gives the game a way to teach visible Japanese
form without a lecture — layers, wrapping, thresholds and ceremony read as
meaning, which fits shrine precincts, packaging, and the layered structure
of a commemorative world.

## 4. Neil Gordon MUNRO (1863–1942) — the resident archaeologist of Ainu life

Scottish physician who went to Japan in the 1890s and stayed almost fifty
years, for years medical director of the Yokohama General Hospital. He was
one of the first to excavate Japan's **prehistoric shell mounds** (Omori,
near Tokyo) and — crucially — he moved to **Nibutani, Hokkaido** to live
among the Ainu, documenting their daily life, beliefs and material culture
at first hand. His *Ainu Creed and Cult* (posthumous, 1962) is still cited
for Ainu ritual, and his collection survives in Japanese museums.

**Why he matters here:** the Ainu religion material in §4 of
`research.md` (kamuy, iyomante, inau) rests on recorders like him. He is
also a caution: he documented a living culture as it was being displaced,
and his framing was of his time.

## 5. Junko HABU (b. 1958) — Jomon archaeology, the deep past

Japanese archaeologist and anthropologist, professor at the University of
California, Berkeley. She works on **Jomon hunter-gatherers**
(ca. 14,000–500 BC) and on the long-term sustainability of hunter-gatherer
societies, combining excavation with ecological and household-level
modelling; also writes on cultural heritage and community-based
archaeology in Japan.

**Why she matters here:** the deeper past the game sits on. If survivalm
ever gestures at who lived on the land before any named group — or at how
a small community sustains itself in a forested archipelago — her work is
the sober reference, and she is one of the younger generations still
producing it.

## 6. Augustin BERQUE (b. 1942) — the geographer of landscape

French geographer, Orientalist and philosopher, born in Rabat; one of the
leading European Japanologists, awarded the Fukuoka Prize (2009). He lived
and taught in Japan and theorised the **relationship between a society and
its landscape**: *Le sauvage et l'artifice: les Japonais devant la nature*
(1986) argues that Japanese landscape is neither untouched nature nor pure
artifice, but a cultivated "third" — the *fûdo* (milieu) tradition he
inherited from the philosopher Watsuji Tetsurō.

**Why he matters here:** he is the geographer for a game whose whole map
*is* a Japan-shaped landscape. His idea that landscape is co-produced by
people is the intellectual justification for a world built to commemorate
a country: terrain as memory.

## 7. Hirokazu MIYAZAKI (b. 1968) — hope as method

Japanese-born (Tokyo) cultural anthropologist, professor at Cornell
University and formerly director of its East Asia Program; trained in
Japan and at the Australian National University. He is one of the most
influential Japanese-born anthropologists of his generation, known for
making a philosophical problem into an ethnographic method: *The Method of
Hope: Anthropology, Philosophy, and Fijian Knowledge* (2004) asks how
people keep hope alive when the outcome is not in their hands, studied
among the Suvavou people of Fiji pursuing a land claim. He has since
worked on money and finance, on Japanese society facing disaster and
decline, and on **Ainu land rights and heritage** in Hokkaido — including
the politics around the Upopoy national museum. He has been a leading
editor in the field (American Ethnologist) and is a public voice in Japan
on why a society needs a different kind of hope.

**Why he matters here:** he is the bridge between the game's premise and
the present. A world built to commemorate a country is an act of hope —
that something survives being remembered — and MIYAZAKI is the
anthropologist who has thought hardest about what that actually means,
including for the Ainu, whose commemoration has been contested. He is also
a direct source for how the game should handle Upopoy-era Ainu politics:
as a live claim, not a museum label.

## 8. Chizuko UENO (b. 1948) — Japan's best-known feminist

Japanese sociologist and feminist scholar, professor emerita of the
University of Tokyo, born in Toyama Prefecture. Often called Japan's
"best-known feminist", she writes on patriarchy, the family, care work,
gender and capitalism, and is a prominent public intellectual — her 2019
University of Tokyo entrance-ceremony speech about inequality became
internationally famous. She is also one of the country's **most
controversial** scholars: her positions on the "comfort women" system,
the imperial succession and wartime responsibility have made her the
target of sustained nationalist attack campaigns.

**Why she matters here:** she is the corrective to a game about traditions.
A commemorative Japan made only of torii, haiku and kamuy freezes the
country as beautiful and harmonious; UENO is the scholar who insists on
asking who does the work, who is excluded, and whose memory the monument
serves — questions the game's puzzles should eventually be able to ask
without becoming a lecture.

## 9. Shinji MIYADAI (b. 1959) — the controversial voice on youth and culture

Japanese sociologist, born in Sendai, professor at Tokyo Metropolitan
University. Trained as a sociologist (and formerly a film director's
assistant), he became one of Japan's most visible public intellectuals on
youth, sexuality, otaku culture, media and the 1995 Aum Shinrikyō attacks,
with a fast-talking, deliberately provocative public style. He has also
been a **controversial** figure — his writings on teenage sexuality and
his media-savvy take on subculture drew heavy criticism, and in 2022 he
survived a knife attack on campus by a man who had written him as a
target.

**Why he matters here:** the counterweight to the "traditional Japan"
material. His work shows Japan as contemporary, urban, unsettled and
globally entangled — the register the game should reach for when its
commemorated Japan starts conflicting with the Japan the player thinks
they know.

## 10. William P. MALM (1928–2024) — the scholar of the shamisen

American ethnomusicologist, professor at the University of Michigan, and
the central figure in Western scholarship on Japanese traditional music.
His research area was **shamisen (三味線, shamisen; the three-string
plucked lute)** music, above all *nagauta* — the "long song" that
accompanies kabuki — documented in *Nagauta: The Heart of Kabuki Music*
(1963), still the standard work. He wrote the standard English survey
*Japanese Music and Musical Instruments* (1959) and was active in the
Society for Ethnomusicology, whose history interview series records his
career.

**Why he matters here:** the shamisen is the instrument most people
recognise as "Japanese music", and its repertoire — nagauta, jiuta,
kabuki, bunraku — is where a commemoration of Japan would most obviously
put music. He is also the methodological model for the game's audio
layer: document an instrument, its repertoire and its social settings
together, not as a sample pack. For the Ainu tonkori and Ryukyuan
sanshin — the other string traditions the game touches — the same
attention applies.

## Notes

- Selection criteria: real, well-documented researchers on Japan, mixing
  classic and current, Japanese and non-Japanese, with at least one
  archaeologist (MUNRO, HABU) and one geographer (BERQUE).
- Women: BENEDICT, OHNUKI-TIERNEY, HENDRY, HABU, UENO. Younger generation:
  HABU (b. 1958), MIYAZAKI (b. 1968).
- Japanese-born: OHNUKI-TIERNEY, HABU, MIYAZAKI, UENO, MIYADAI.
- Controversial figures: UENO, MIYADAI (both included on purpose — a
  commemorative world should carry its tensions, not just its treasures).
- Spelling note: the instrument is **shamisen** (三味線), not "shamizen";
  the Okinawan relative is the **sanshin** (三線), the Ainu relative the
  **tonkori**. In-game text should use these forms.
- Deliberately omitted: early Meiji-era "Japan hands" whose framing was
  orientalist without fieldwork, and researchers whose main reputations
  rest on discredited work.
- Every Japanese term used in the game should be traceable to a source in
  this file or in `research.md`.
